On September 5, 2026, German company Isar Aerospace launched its two-stage Spectrum rocket from Andøya Spaceport in Norway, successfully delivering five small satellites and an experimental payload into low Earth orbit. It became the first privately developed rocket to reach orbit from continental Europe. This milestone gives Europe a sovereign, commercially operated route to space outside the traditional Arianespace arrangement, which launches from French Guiana rather than European soil. The 28-meter-tall Spectrum lifted off at 10:12 pm CEST and reached an elliptical orbit about seven minutes after launch. The rocket is designed to deliver up to 1,000 kilograms to low Earth orbit, and most of its development and manufacturing, including its Aquila engines, was done in-house.

In 2025, Isar Aerospace was selected for the European Launcher Challenge, which requires providers to achieve an orbital launch before 2027.

In September 2026, Terence Eden published a post, amplified by Simon Willison, citing an Interisle report that found about 85 million new generic top-level domain (gTLD) registrations in 2025, of which at least 8.5 million were added to blocklists by May 2025. Eden argues that the abuse rate floor is 10% and likely closer to 20%. If even the lower estimate holds, roughly one in ten—and possibly one in five—newly registered generic domains are tied to criminal activity, making DNS abuse a systemic trust and safety problem. This puts pressure on ICANN, registries, and registrars to move from voluntary measures to enforceable policies that protect consumers and brands. The statistics focus on gTLDs, excluding country-code top-level domains, and measure blocklist additions rather than confirmed crimes, so the true abuse rate may differ. rss · Simon Willison · Sep 6, 14:40

Generic top-level domains (gTLDs) are domain extensions not tied to any country—examples include .com, .org, and many newer suffixes—managed under ICANN's multi-stakeholder framework. DNS abuse refers to harmful activity such as phishing, malware distribution, and botnet operations that relies on or targets the DNS infrastructure. ICANN oversees domain registration through contracts with registries and registrars, but definitions of abuse and who should curb it remain contentious.

## [Why Rewriting Legacy Systems from Scratch Usually Fails](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Sep/6/theres-no-limit-to-how-bad-code-can-get/) ⭐️ 7.0/10

Simon Willison posted a comment on Lobste.rs arguing that greenfield rewrites of legacy systems rarely work, based on his own engineering experience. He recommends shoring up the old system with automated testing and targeted refactors instead. This is a widely debated topic in software engineering, and Willison's credibility gives weight to the incremental migration approach. Teams facing overwhelming technical debt may reconsider expensive and risky full rewrites after reading his argument. Willison notes that the old system remains a moving target because it runs core business processes, and its developers lose incentive to maintain quality once a replacement is planned. He links Will Larson's article 'Migrations: the sole scalable fix to tech debt' as the best resource for responsible migration, and warns that failed rewrites can leave two production systems—one janky old system and a new system that is often 80% inactive code.

rss · Simon Willison · Sep 6, 09:08

**Background**: Technical debt refers to the accumulated cost of shortcuts taken during software development, such as messy code or missing tests, which makes future changes harder. A 'greenfield rewrite' means building a new system from scratch to replace a legacy one, while a migration or incremental refactor improves the existing system step by step. Many teams attempt rewrites when technical debt becomes unbearable, but they often underestimate the hidden complexity in the old system's behavior and scope.

**Discussion**: In the original Lobste.rs thread, another commenter suggested 'burning it down' and rewriting from scratch when technical debt becomes overwhelming. Willison's response pushes back against that idea, and his widely shared comment reflects a growing sentiment in the engineering community that incremental migration is often safer than a full rewrite.

## [F-Droid Proposes Debian AI Policy, No Ban and Contributor Responsibility](https://gitlab.com/fdroid/admin/-/work_items/699) ⭐️ 7.0/10

F-Droid has proposed in GitLab work item 699 to adopt a slightly modified version of Debian's newly passed 'Responsible Use of Generative AI' policy as a temporary policy. The policy neither endorses nor bans generative AI in development, maintenance, and documentation, following the option that won Debian's General Resolution vote. F-Droid is one of the most widely used repositories for free and open-source Android apps, so its policy choice can influence how the broader FOSS community handles generative AI. The proposal offers a balanced precedent: allow AI tools for productivity while keeping contributors accountable for quality, correctness, and legal compliance. The proposed temporary policy explicitly states that using AI does not reduce contributor responsibility, and all submissions must still meet the same quality, correctness, maintainability, and legal compliance standards. Disclosure of AI use is encouraged but not required, matching the language of Debian's policy adopted on August 28, 2026.

## [China Grants Conditional Approval to First AI-Assisted COVID-19 Drug](https://www.gelonghui.com/live/2653282) ⭐️ 7.0/10

China's National Medical Products Administration has conditionally approved Eisitwevir hydrochloride tablets (brand name: Aipusiwei), the country's first original drug developed with AI assistance, for treating mild-to-moderate COVID-19 in adults. The drug, jointly developed by Westlake University, Westlake Laboratory, and Westlake Pharmaceuticals (Hangzhou), took only three and a half years from initial discovery to completion of clinical trials. This approval validates AI's potential to shorten the drug discovery timeline and marks a key milestone in AI-driven pharmaceutical innovation in China. It may encourage more domestic biotech companies to invest in AI-assisted drug research and development, particularly in infectious disease and other urgent therapeutic areas. Conditional approval requires the developer to conduct confirmatory post-marketing studies to verify the drug's clinical benefit. The indication is limited to adults with mild-to-moderate COVID-19, and the entire journey from target discovery to clinical trial completion took roughly three and a half years.
