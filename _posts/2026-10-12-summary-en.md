## [Terence Tao's 'Math 2.0' Slides: How AI Is Reshaping Mathematics](https://teorth.github.io/tao-web/slides/math-2-0-caltech-2026.pdf) ⭐️ 8.0/10

Terence Tao, a Fields Medalist and professor of mathematics at UCLA, published a slide deck titled "Math 2.0" (dated October 2026, accompanying his October 10 blog post of the same name) arguing that AI has ended the era in which mathematical proofs were scarce and that the field must move beyond the automated solving of open problems. The talk describes the historical "Math 1.0" state of mathematics, the current state induced by an excessive emphasis on AI-driven problem solving, and his vision for a future "Math 2.0" in which AI tools are used to sustain both the internal mathematical community and the role mathematical understanding plays in the wider world. Coming from one of the world's most prominent mathematicians, this framing signals that AI is not merely a new tool but a structural disruption to how mathematics is practiced, funded and taught. It touches on practical consequences such as grant proposals that must be rewritten once an AI solves the problem they were written for, the morale of researchers who lose long-standing problems overnight, and how other scientific fields — including medicine — should think about AI systems whose outputs are useful but not fully understood. The deck is a set of talk slides rather than a formal paper, and its slide on cancer medicine drew criticism from commenters who work in drug discovery, who argued that it misrepresents how much mechanistic understanding medicine actually requires. A recurring technical theme in the discussion is that current math AI is not a "good citizen" of the mathematical corpus: it produces solutions but rarely makes elegant, reusable additions to theory, definitions and proofs.

**Background**: Terence Tao is an Australian-American mathematician at UCLA who won the Fields Medal in 2006 for contributions to partial differential equations, combinatorics, harmonic analysis and additive number theory, and is widely regarded as one of the leading mathematicians of his generation. Open problems are questions that the mathematical community has not yet resolved and that traditionally serve as landmarks guiding research; Tao's phrase "open problems are lighthouses, not destinations" reframes them as pointers to where theory needs to be extended. The talk responds to a wave of reports in 2026 that frontier AI models had solved long-standing research problems, which prompted debate about whether mathematics should optimize for finding proofs or for understanding them.

## [OpenAI Releases 722 AI-Generated Math Manuscripts From Internal Frontier Model](https://t.me/zaihuapd/44351) ⭐️ 8.0/10

OpenAI published a GitHub collection containing 722 manuscripts and 372 result series on long-unsolved mathematical problems, all generated by an unreleased internal frontier model, with many of the proofs formalized and checked in Lean. The repository states the model consumed roughly 3 hours of ChatGPT Pro thinking compute per result and attempted about 4,000 problems during evaluation, and OpenAI also released 10 reasoning summaries. If the results hold up, this is a striking signal that frontier AI models are moving from solving textbook exercises to contributing to genuinely open research mathematics, which could change how mathematicians attack unsolved problems and how AI math benchmarks are designed. It also raises the stakes for verification practices, since unverified machine-generated proofs could flood the literature and the arXiv. The collection mixes machine-generated manuscripts with proof artifacts, and many but not all proofs have been formalized in Lean, meaning some results remain unverified and should be treated as claims rather than established theorems. The compute figures — about 3 hours of ChatGPT Pro thinking per result across roughly 4,000 attempted problems — give a rare glimpse into the inference budget behind frontier-model mathematical reasoning.

**Background**: Lean is an open-source proof assistant and functional programming language, based on the calculus of inductive constructions, that lets mathematicians write proofs in a form a computer can mechanically check; this process is called formal verification. In mathematics, a proof written only in prose can hide gaps, whereas a Lean-formalized proof removes that ambiguity. The term "frontier model" refers to the most advanced, resource-intensive AI models, such as OpenAI's GPT series, which are typically trained at costs of hundreds of millions of dollars, and "ChatGPT Pro thinking compute" refers to the extended-reasoning mode available in OpenAI's paid Pro subscription.

Commenters debated whether agentic LLMs can genuinely replace weeks or months of complex software work, and how deeply experts' and the public's perceptions of model limits have diverged. The thread highlights a growing credibility and communication problem in the AI industry: if researchers, practitioners, and the general public hold radically different expectations about what LLM agents can do, both over-hyped deployments and unfounded dismissal become more likely. This matters for how enterprises budget for AI tooling, how tooling vendors market agentic products, and how developers decide whether to trust AI-generated code in production. A key counterargument in the thread was that even the top ~0.2% of practitioners are not actually unanimous about agents completing large, complex projects from a single prompt; the real divide is between those who understand codebases are needed for maintenance, future token costs, optimization and code erosion, and those who don't review code and simply assume the AI got it right. Several commenters also noted that LLM agents remain constrained by hallucinations, statelessness and context limits, and that solving frontier math or chess says more about those narrow domains than about general intelligence.

## ["Passkeys Are a Trap" Essay Sparks Heated Authentication Debate](https://nonfunctional.substack.com/p/passkeys-are-a-trap-do-not-use-them) ⭐️ 7.0/10

The article makes an opinionated case against passkeys, and commenters pushed back on both its framing and its substance — with one noting that the piece's own final addendum concedes passkeys are domain-bound and therefore resistant to man-in-the-middle attacks. Passkeys are being pushed by Apple, Google, Microsoft and the FIDO Alliance as the default replacement for passwords, so criticism of lock-in and attestation touches every developer and user who is being nudged toward them. If portability and user-controlled credential strength are not solved, adoption could stall or fragment across ecosystems. A key technical point raised is that attestation can only be demanded at credential registration — it cannot be retroactively required at login — and browsers surface a permission prompt when a site asks for it, so the feared rejection of non-attested authenticators depends entirely on whether services like Netflix or Twitter actually enforce it at signup. Commenters also flagged that 1Password recently began enforcing userVerification: required, which broke passkey use on desktops lacking TouchID.

hackernews · laurex · Oct 11, 21:59 · [Discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50048312)

**Background**: Passkeys are FIDO2/WebAuthn credentials built on public-key cryptography: the private key stays on a device or in a synced keychain such as iCloud Keychain or Google Password Manager, and the server only ever stores the public key. Because each credential is bound to an origin (domain), it resists phishing and man-in-the-middle attacks in a way passwords do not. Attestation is an optional mechanism in which the authenticator proves its make and model to the relying party, which can be used for policy enforcement but also enables vendor lock-in and device fingerprinting. Portability remains contested because exporting passkeys between platforms is limited, unlike passwords stored in a manager the user controls.

**Discussion**: Sentiment is split: security-minded commenters defended passkeys, with one saying they abolished passwords for their org's internal systems, while others objected to the alarmist title even as they conceded a "kernel of truth." Portability and control were the dominant concerns — one user refuses to enable passkeys because they want to back up credentials in a safe and choose per-service password strength, distrusting a phone thumbprint. Another critic highlighted that the essay's final addendum admits to having made a compelling case for passkeys and having omitted domain-binding, its most important anti-phishing feature.

## [NYC's "Click to Cancel" rule for subscriptions takes effect](https://www.nyc.gov/main/click-to-cancel) ⭐️ 7.0/10

New York City's "Click to Cancel" rule is now in effect, requiring businesses that sell subscriptions to let consumers cancel through a method as simple as the one they used to sign up — typically a single click or an equivalently easy step. The rule targets the dark patterns and convoluted cancellation flows that have long trapped subscribers in unwanted recurring charges. Subscription-based businesses are a huge and growing part of the consumer economy, and cancellation friction is one of their core retention levers, so a rule in the largest U.S. city can force product and billing-flow changes well beyond New York. It also adds momentum to a broader regulatory push, alongside California's existing law and similar legislation being prepared in the EU. The rule governs the cancellation experience itself, so the practical open question is enforcement — how aggressively the city will pursue violators once public attention fades. It also does nothing to stop businesses from reducing the value of cancellation through other means, such as early-termination fees or lock-in clauses buried in lengthy terms of service.

hackernews · gregsadetsky · Oct 11, 18:01 · [Discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50045946)

**Background**: "Click to cancel" is a consumer-protection principle holding that ending a subscription should be no harder than starting one, in contrast to common practices like requiring phone calls, retention chat mazes, or hidden account settings. California has already enacted a similar rule, and the U.S. Federal Trade Commission has pursued an equivalent nationwide measure, so New York City is joining an existing patchwork of rules rather than creating an entirely new concept.

**Discussion**: Commenters broadly welcomed the rule but focused on enforcement: one long-time resident doubted NYC's follow-through on consumer rules, while another pointed to Adobe as an example of a company that offers click-to-cancel but charges a cancellation fee equal to the remaining term, and predicted more businesses will copy that workaround. Others noted California has had a similar rule for years and called for a federal version, and one commenter credited the current mayor's agenda; a self-described EU digital policy analyst said the European Commission is expected to table a "Digital Fairness Act" in November containing the same click-to-cancel principle.

## [Japan Declares Cyber Emergency After Attacks Hit 20+ Major Firms](https://www.ft.com/content/2d4333d1-8119-44a4-a5a5-2ee9ef2544fa?syn-25a6b1a6=1) ⭐️ 7.0/10

The Japanese government declared a "cyber emergency" after more than 20 major companies spanning rail, automotive, healthcare and government facilities were attacked in recent weeks, potentially exposing tens of millions of customer records. JR East said its ticket-booking system may have leaked the email addresses of 1.67 million customers, and several incidents have been linked to a ransomware attack on SoftBank subsidiary IDC Frontier. This is a rare national-level escalation that turns a string of corporate breaches into a governance and regulatory problem: Japan's Financial Services Agency has ordered banks to stop accepting driver's licenses as identity proof for new accounts and to require chip-bearing ID documents instead. The fallout touches banks, critical infrastructure operators and ordinary consumers, and it adds momentum to the debate over how AI is lowering the bar for sophisticated attacks. The incident chain is anchored in a ransomware attack on IDC Frontier, a SoftBank subsidiary whose IDCF Cloud platform was shut down, with reports saying 495 Japanese companies and local governments were affected and that some customers were warned their data might be unrecoverable. Experts describe Japan's defenses as weak and say AI is lowering the barrier to complex attacks, but there is no evidence yet linking the latest wave specifically to AI.

IDC Frontier is a SoftBank-owned data center and cloud provider that hosts services for many Japanese companies and municipal governments. ## [UC Berkeley Study: 10 Minutes of AI Use Erodes Persistence on Hard Tasks](https://news.berkeley.edu/2026/10/09/using-ai-for-just-10-minutes-erodes-your-ability-to-persist-at-hard-things/) ⭐️ 7.0/10

A study involving UC Berkeley researchers, with 1,222 participants, found that after using AI tools such as ChatGPT for just 10 minutes on fraction arithmetic and SAT reading-comprehension problems, participants' accuracy and persistence dropped sharply once the tool was removed — some answered incorrectly or simply gave up. The researchers recommend designing AI to act more as a tutor than as a system that completes tasks on the user's behalf. The finding suggests that even very short exposure to AI assistance can carry a measurable cognitive cost, which matters for schools, universities and workplaces now rushing to embed AI into learning and productivity workflows. It also pushes back on the assumption that AI tools are always a net boost to performance, arguing that how the tool is framed — tutor versus task-completer — may determine whether users retain their own skills. The experiment used a relatively large sample of 1,222 participants and two task types — fraction calculation problems and SAT reading comprehension — with AI help first and then withdrawn to measure persistence and accuracy. Notably, the negative effect appeared after only about 10 minutes of AI use, and the study's authors frame tutoring-style AI interaction rather than direct answer-giving as the safer design.

telegram · zaihuapd · Oct 11, 11:28

**Background**: Large language model assistants like ChatGPT can answer questions, explain concepts or write text on demand, which has made them popular study and work aids. Cognitive scientists have long studied 'cognitive offloading', where people delegate mental work to external tools and may become less practiced at the underlying skill. This study tests that idea directly by providing AI help and then taking it away, using persistence — the willingness to keep working on a hard problem — as the key measure. SAT reading comprehension is a standard US college-admissions test section, used here as a familiar benchmark of difficult verbal reasoning.
